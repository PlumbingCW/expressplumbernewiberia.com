Tankless Water Heater in an Attic: What New Iberia, LA Homes Need to Know

An attic can hold a tankless unit, but heat, venting, a drain pan and access all matter. Here is how to plan the install.

Plumber on call.

Many south Louisiana homes sit on slabs, so the water heater often ends up in a closet, a garage or the attic. A tankless unit takes less space than a tank, which makes the attic tempting. It can work, but the attic brings its own challenges that you should plan for before you buy.

Why people choose the attic

A slab house has no basement or crawlspace, and a garage may already be crowded. A wall-mounted tankless unit frees floor space, and the attic keeps it out of the way. In some homes the existing tank is already there, so the supply lines are close.

The challenges of an attic install

Heat.

Attic temperatures in a Louisiana summer are severe. Heat stresses electronics and shortens the life of a unit that is not rated for it.

Venting.

Tankless gas units need a specific vent material and path to the roof or an outside wall, with proper clearances.

Drain pan.

A leak above a ceiling does far more damage than one on a garage floor, so a pan with a drain line is essential.

Access.

Service needs a safe, lit path and a working platform. Annual flushing means a plumber has to reach the unit comfortably.

What to ask before you decide

A short checklist

  • Is the unit rated for the temperatures it will see in the attic?
  • Is there a safe, floored path and a lit work platform at the unit?
  • Where does the vent run, and does it meet the maker's clearances?
  • Is the gas line big enough for the unit's demand?
  • Is there a drain pan with a line to a safe outlet?

Gas or electric in the attic

Electric tankless units need no vent but draw a large electrical load, and the panel may need an upgrade. Gas units need a bigger gas line and a vent. The right answer depends on what your house already has. A plumber who looks at the space and the supply can tell you which route costs less and works better.

For the install side, read tankless water heater service. If you are weighing a standard tank instead, see water heater installation.

Protecting the ceiling below

The biggest risk of an attic install is water. A tank unit leaks slowly as it fails, and a tankless unit can leak at a fitting, a scale-clogged heat exchanger or a failed relief valve. In either case the water lands on insulation and drywall, and the stain on the ceiling is often the first sign. A pan under the unit, with a drain line that exits somewhere you will see it, turns that into a minor event.

Some homeowners add a leak sensor with an automatic shutoff valve on the supply. It is an inexpensive add-on that can stop a small leak before it grows.

Lighting, floor and a safe way up

Service people need to reach the unit without stepping on ceiling joists. A solid floored path and a working platform around the heater are as important as the unit itself. If the access is a pull-down ladder in a hallway, think about how a plumber will carry parts and a flush kit through it. Plan this before the unit goes in, because retrofitting a platform later costs more.

Finally, check with your insurer. Some policies treat water damage from an attic appliance differently, and a leak sensor or a pan with a drain line can help your case if something fails. A plumber can document the install with photos, which is useful for a claim later.
